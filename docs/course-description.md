# Evolution: Natural Selection and the Tree of Life

**Title:** Evolution: Natural Selection and the Tree of Life

**Target Audience:** High school students (grades 9–12), no prior biology required

**Prerequisites:** None. Students are not assumed to have taken any prior
biology course. Basic reading comprehension and curiosity about the natural
world are the only requirements. Comfort with graphs and simple ratios is
helpful but not required.

---

## Course Overview

How did the astonishing diversity of life on Earth come to be? Why do penguins
and polar bears both have thick insulating layers even though they live on
opposite ends of the planet? Why do humans share 98% of their DNA with
chimpanzees? Why does flu season require a new vaccine every year? The answer
to all of these questions — and thousands more — is evolution.

This course is a deep, engaging exploration of biological evolution: the
central organizing idea of all of modern biology. Students will learn how
Charles Darwin's voyages sparked a revolution in scientific thinking, how the
four simple conditions of natural selection can explain the origin of every
living thing on Earth, and how scientists trace the family tree of life from
the first single-celled microbes to modern humans. The course places special
emphasis on **interactive learning**: every major concept is paired with a
hands-on MicroSim — a browser-based simulation that lets students manipulate
variables, observe outcomes, and build intuition by experimentation rather than
memorization alone.

By the end of this course, students will understand evolution not as a
collection of facts to memorize, but as a powerful explanatory framework they
can apply to medicine, conservation, agriculture, and the everyday world around
them. No prior biology background is assumed; the course builds all necessary
foundations from the ground up, making it accessible to complete beginners
while remaining deep enough to satisfy curious, advanced learners.

## Main Topics Covered

1. **The Nature of Science** — what science is, how hypotheses become theories,
   how evidence is evaluated, and why evolution is one of the most
   well-supported scientific theories in history
2. **Variation and Heredity** — what traits are, how they are passed from
   parents to offspring, how mutations introduce new variation, and why heritable
   variation is the essential raw material for evolution
3. **Natural Selection** — Darwin's four conditions, fitness, adaptation,
   and how natural selection acts as a sorting mechanism on variation
4. **Modes of Selection and Other Evolutionary Forces** — directional,
   stabilizing, and disruptive selection; sexual selection; genetic drift;
   gene flow; and how these forces interact to shape populations
5. **Evidence for Evolution** — the fossil record, comparative anatomy
   (homologous and analogous structures, vestigial organs), biogeography,
   and molecular evidence from DNA and protein sequences
6. **Population Genetics and Microevolution** — allele frequencies, gene
   pools, the Hardy-Weinberg principle, and how populations change over
   generations
7. **Speciation and Macroevolution** — the biological species concept,
   reproductive isolation, allopatric and sympatric speciation, adaptive
   radiation, convergent evolution, and extinction
8. **Phylogenetics and the Tree of Life** — how scientists reconstruct
   evolutionary relationships, how to read and build cladograms and
   phylogenetic trees, and the major domains and kingdoms of life
9. **Major Evolutionary Transitions** — the origin of life, the rise of
   eukaryotes, the evolution of multicellularity, the colonization of land,
   the origin of flight, and other landmark events in life's history
10. **Human Evolution** — the hominin fossil record, the evolution of the
    genus *Homo*, the origin of *Homo sapiens*, and what makes humans
    evolutionarily unique
11. **Coevolution and Evolutionary Ecology** — predator–prey arms races,
    host–parasite coevolution, mutualism, and how species shape each other's
    evolution over time
12. **Evolution in Action** — antibiotic resistance, viral evolution, artificial
    selection and domestication, and real-time observable evolution in
    the modern world
13. **Applications of Evolutionary Thinking** — how evolutionary biology
    informs medicine, vaccine design, conservation biology, agriculture,
    and our understanding of human behavior

## Topics Not Covered

To keep the course focused, the following topics are explicitly out of scope:

- **Detailed cell biology and biochemistry** (cell membrane structure, ATP
  synthesis, photosynthesis, cellular respiration) — these are covered in a
  general biology course
- **Mendelian genetics in depth** (Punnett squares, linkage, epistasis,
  chromosomal inheritance) — only the concepts of heritable variation and
  mutation are introduced as they relate to evolution
- **Detailed molecular biology mechanisms** (transcription, translation, DNA
  replication machinery) — molecular evidence is used but the mechanisms are
  not the focus
- **Ecological community dynamics** (food webs, nutrient cycles, ecosystem
  services) — coevolution and selection pressures are covered, but full
  ecology is a separate course
- **Human anatomy and physiology** — human biology is discussed only through
  an evolutionary lens (comparative anatomy, vestigial structures, hominin
  evolution)
- **Plant biology or microbiology as stand-alone subjects** — both are
  discussed as branches of the tree of life, not studied in depth on their own

## Interactive MicroSims

This course is built around **interactive browser-based MicroSims** — small
simulations that let students explore evolutionary concepts by changing
parameters and observing outcomes in real time. Representative MicroSims
include:

- **Natural Selection Sandbox** — adjust mutation rate, population size, and
  selection pressure to watch allele frequencies shift over generations
- **Peppered Moth Simulator** — change industrial pollution levels and watch
  camouflage drive population color change
- **Genetic Drift Explorer** — compare large and small populations to see how
  chance events fix or eliminate alleles
- **Hardy-Weinberg Calculator** — input allele frequencies and verify whether
  a population is evolving
- **Speciation Timeline** — apply geographic barriers, selection pressures, and
  time to watch one population split into two species
- **Tree of Life Navigator** — explore the full tree of life interactively,
  zooming from domain level down to individual species
- **Phylogenetic Tree Builder** — drag and drop traits to construct cladograms
  and test competing evolutionary hypotheses
- **Fossil Record Timeline** — scroll through 4 billion years and place key
  organisms at their first appearance in the fossil record
- **Coevolution Arms Race** — watch predator and prey traits race against each
  other across generations
- **Antibiotic Resistance Simulator** — run a bacterial population through
  antibiotic treatments and watch resistance evolve in real time
- **Human Skull Comparison** — rotate and compare hominin skulls across 7
  million years of evolution
- **Artificial Selection Lab** — breed virtual organisms for specific traits
  and observe the speed of trait change compared to natural selection

## Learning Outcomes

After completing this course, students will be able to:

### Remember
*Retrieving, recognizing, and recalling relevant knowledge from long-term memory.*

- Recall the four conditions required for natural selection to occur
  (variation, heritability, differential survival/reproduction, and selection
  pressure)
- Name the major types of natural selection (directional, stabilizing,
  disruptive, and sexual selection) and give an example of each
- List the five main categories of evidence that support the theory of
  evolution (fossil record, comparative anatomy, biogeography, molecular
  evidence, and direct observation)
- Recall the definitions of key vocabulary: allele, gene pool, fitness,
  adaptation, speciation, clade, phylogeny, homologous structure, vestigial
  organ, genetic drift, gene flow, and reproductive isolation
- Identify the major domains and kingdoms of life on the tree of life
- Name the major hominin species in the human evolutionary lineage and their
  approximate dates
- Recall the Hardy-Weinberg principle and state the five conditions under
  which a population is NOT evolving
- Identify Charles Darwin, Alfred Russel Wallace, and other key figures in
  the history of evolutionary thought

### Understand
*Constructing meaning from instructional messages, including oral, written, and graphic communication.*

- Explain in plain language why heritable variation is necessary for natural
  selection to produce evolutionary change
- Describe how genetic mutations, sexual reproduction, and recombination each
  contribute to the variation that natural selection acts upon
- Explain the difference between microevolution (changes in allele frequency
  within a population) and macroevolution (speciation, extinction, and major
  body-plan changes)
- Interpret a phylogenetic tree or cladogram to identify common ancestors,
  sister groups, and the relative timing of evolutionary divergence
- Explain why analogous structures (like a bat wing and an insect wing) are
  not evidence of common ancestry, while homologous structures are
- Summarize the endosymbiotic theory for the origin of mitochondria and
  chloroplasts and identify the evidence that supports it
- Explain why antibiotic resistance and influenza vaccine updates are
  practical demonstrations of evolution by natural selection
- Describe how geographic isolation can lead to allopatric speciation, and
  contrast this with sympatric speciation
- Explain how the Hardy-Weinberg equation serves as a null model for detecting
  evolution in a population
- Distinguish between genetic drift and natural selection as evolutionary
  forces, and explain when drift has a stronger effect than selection

### Apply
*Carrying out or using a procedure in a given situation.*

- Use a MicroSim to adjust selection pressure, mutation rate, and population
  size and predict how allele frequencies will change over time
- Read an unfamiliar phylogenetic tree and correctly answer questions about
  relatedness, common ancestors, and character evolution
- Apply the four conditions of natural selection to a novel scenario (e.g.,
  antibiotic resistance, beak variation in birds, camouflage in moths) and
  predict the direction of evolutionary change
- Use the Hardy-Weinberg equation to calculate expected genotype frequencies
  from observed allele frequencies and determine whether a population is
  evolving
- Place major evolutionary events (origin of life, eukaryotes, animals, land
  plants, dinosaurs, mammals, humans) correctly on a geological time scale
- Use comparative DNA or protein sequence data to infer which of several
  species are most closely related
- Apply the concept of reproductive isolation to determine whether two
  populations have speciated or remain one species
- Use a phylogenetic tree builder MicroSim to construct a cladogram from a
  trait matrix and verify it against a known reference tree

### Analyze
*Breaking material into constituent parts and determining how the parts relate to one another and to an overall structure or purpose.*

- Analyze a dataset of phenotypic variation in a population and determine
  which type of selection (directional, stabilizing, or disruptive) is most
  likely operating
- Compare the rates of evolution in organisms with different generation times
  (bacteria vs. elephants) and explain the underlying mechanisms
- Examine conflicting phylogenetic hypotheses and evaluate which is better
  supported by morphological vs. molecular evidence
- Analyze a case study of coevolution (e.g., flowering plants and
  pollinators, or cheetahs and gazelles) and identify the reciprocal
  selection pressures driving each lineage's evolution
- Break down a claim about human evolution and distinguish what the fossil
  and genetic evidence does and does not support
- Compare artificial selection (domestication) with natural selection and
  identify the similarities and differences in mechanism and speed
- Analyze the conditions under which genetic drift is expected to overpower
  natural selection, using population size as the key variable
- Examine the tree of life at multiple scales (domain, kingdom, phylum,
  species) using the Tree of Life MicroSim and identify patterns in
  diversity, extinction, and adaptive radiation

### Evaluate
*Making judgments based on criteria and standards through checking and critiquing.*

- Evaluate common misconceptions about evolution (e.g., "evolution has a
  direction or goal," "organisms evolve because they need to," "humans
  evolved from chimpanzees") and articulate precisely why each is incorrect
- Assess the strength of evidence for evolution from multiple independent
  lines (fossil record, molecular data, biogeography) and explain why
  convergence across disciplines makes the theory especially robust
- Critique a flawed phylogenetic tree and identify which groupings are
  paraphyletic or polyphyletic, explaining why they misrepresent
  evolutionary history
- Judge whether a proposed mechanism for a new trait (e.g., the evolution of
  the eye, or flight) is plausible given what we know about gradual selection
  on intermediate forms
- Evaluate the real-world consequences of failing to apply evolutionary
  thinking in medicine (e.g., incomplete antibiotic courses, resistance
  management) and propose evidence-based alternatives
- Assess the ethical implications of applying knowledge of human evolutionary
  history to questions about race, intelligence, and human behavior, and
  explain why the evolutionary evidence does not support racial hierarchies
- Evaluate the quality and limitations of different types of evolutionary
  evidence (e.g., why the fossil record is incomplete, why molecular clocks
  have uncertainty) and explain how scientists account for these limitations

### Create
*Putting elements together to form a coherent or functional whole; reorganizing elements into a new pattern or structure.*

- Design a hypothetical organism adapted to a specific extreme environment,
  justifying each trait in terms of the selection pressures present and the
  evolutionary mechanisms that could produce it
- Construct an original phylogenetic tree for a set of 10 organisms using a
  trait matrix, molecular sequence data, or both, and present it with a
  written defense of the groupings chosen
- Produce an illustrated evolutionary timeline for a chosen lineage (e.g.,
  whales, horses, birds, flowering plants) that incorporates fossil evidence,
  molecular data, and key selection pressures at each stage
- Develop an original lesson or interactive explanation of one evolutionary
  concept (natural selection, genetic drift, speciation) aimed at a
  middle-school audience, using analogy, visualization, or a hands-on activity
- Create a research proposal for a field or laboratory study that would test
  an evolutionary hypothesis about a local organism, including a prediction,
  method, and explanation of how results would support or falsify the
  hypothesis
- Design parameters for a new MicroSim that would allow users to explore an
  aspect of evolution not covered by existing simulations, specifying the
  variables, outputs, and learning goals
- Synthesize information from multiple units to write an essay arguing for
  (or against) a specific claim about evolution — for example, whether
  punctuated equilibrium or gradualism better describes a particular
  lineage — using evidence from the fossil record, molecular biology, and
  population genetics

## Key Areas of Focus

This intelligent textbook is organized around four cross-cutting skills that
students will develop and apply throughout every unit:

1. **Critical Thinking** — Students will move beyond recall to question
   assumptions, evaluate evidence, identify logical fallacies, and construct
   well-reasoned arguments about how evolution works and what the evidence
   actually supports. Every chapter poses open-ended questions that require
   students to defend a position with evidence rather than accept claims at
   face value. Students will learn to distinguish the scientific meaning of
   "theory" from its colloquial meaning, and to recognize what counts as
   falsifiable evidence in evolutionary biology.

2. **Systems Thinking** — Evolution is a complex adaptive system: changes in
   allele frequency ripple through population fitness; extinction of one species
   reshapes the selection pressures on others; a single mutation can cascade
   into new body plans over millions of generations. Students will use
   causal-loop reasoning, feedback analysis, and structural mapping to
   understand how the parts of an ecosystem and a genome interact — and why
   evolution so often produces surprising, counter-intuitive outcomes that
   cannot be predicted by studying any single part in isolation.

3. **Understanding Human Cognitive Bias** — Our intuitions about evolution are
   shaped by systematic psychological errors: teleological thinking (the idea
   that organisms evolve "toward" a goal), essentialism (the assumption that
   species have fixed, unchanging natures), and the availability heuristic
   (judging evolutionary timescales by how easy dramatic changes are to
   visualize). Students will learn to recognize these biases in themselves and
   in public discourse about evolution, understand how they fuel popular
   misconceptions, and develop strategies for reasoning more accurately about
   deep time, probability, and gradual change.

4. **Detecting Misinformation** — Evolution is one of the most publicly
   misrepresented scientific theories in existence. Students will practice
   lateral reading, source evaluation, and fact-checking workflows to assess
   claims about evolution encountered in media, social networks, and everyday
   conversation. They will learn to distinguish peer-reviewed scientific
   consensus from fringe claims, identify the rhetorical strategies used to
   manufacture doubt about well-established science, and apply the same
   critical evaluation skills to emerging areas where genuine scientific
   debate does exist — such as the relative importance of different mechanisms
   of evolution beyond natural selection.

## Course Format

- **Chapters:** 13 content chapters plus appendices
- **MicroSims:** 40+ interactive simulations, at least 3 per chapter
- **Assessments:** Chapter quizzes, phylogenetic tree interpretation exercises,
  and a final capstone project
- **Appendices:** Overlap with AP Biology, glossary, references, and a guide
  to further reading

## Why This Course Matters

Evolution is not merely a chapter in a biology textbook — it is the
foundational theory that unifies all of biology. Understanding evolution
explains why doctors prescribe the full course of antibiotics, why
conservationists prioritize genetic diversity in endangered species, why
paleontologists find the same lineages on continents that were once joined,
and why our bodies carry the molecular signatures of ancient single-celled
ancestors. More broadly, understanding how science produces and refines
theories — using evidence, peer review, and the willingness to revise — is one
of the most important intellectual tools a student can develop in a world full
of misinformation. This course equips students not just with biological
knowledge, but with a way of thinking about evidence, change, and complexity
that will serve them across every field they encounter.
